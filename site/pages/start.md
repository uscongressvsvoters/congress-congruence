# Start here

<p class="lede">Americans don't vote on the bills Congress decides. They elect members of the House
and Senate, and those members cast the votes. So what Congress decides can differ from what the people
it represents want. This site exists to show where it does and where it doesn't.</p>

## Why this site exists {#why-this-site}

Members of Congress represent everyone where they live, but voters rarely get to see how a member's
votes line up with what those people actually wanted. Independent surveys sometimes ask Americans how
they would have voted on the same bills Congress voted on. This site puts the two side by side: each
member's vote, and what people in their state or district told those surveys.

The site is run by one volunteer, with no funding, advertising or party connection. It doesn't score
or rank members, and it doesn't say how anyone should have voted. It shows the comparison and leaves
the judgment to you. More on [About](about.html), and how the comparison works on
[Methods](methods.html).

The rest of this page explains how representation works: why voters don't vote on federal laws
themselves, where they do, and the long-running debate over how closely members should follow the
people they represent.

## The short answer {#short}

The Constitution gives the power to make federal laws to Congress, not to voters directly. Its first
article begins: "All legislative Powers herein granted shall be vested in a Congress of the United
States." There is no national ballot for bills, and the United States has never held a national
referendum.

Voters' power over federal laws comes through elections: choosing who sits in Congress and replacing
members whose votes they disagree with. That's why this site compares how members voted with what
the people they represent told independent surveys. Those surveys are one of the few ways to see
what constituents wanted on a specific vote.

## A representative republic {#republic}

The United States is often called a **constitutional republic** and also a **representative
democracy**. Both describe the same system:

- **Representative:** people elect others to make decisions for them, rather than deciding each
  question themselves (that would be **direct democracy**).
- **Republic:** power comes from the people, and there is no monarch. The Constitution promises every
  state "a Republican Form of Government" (Article IV, Section 4).
- **Constitutional:** the government's powers, and limits on them, are set by a written constitution
  that the government can't change on its own.

## Why voters don't vote on federal bills {#why}

**The Constitution doesn't provide for it.** It sets out how a bill becomes law: passing both the
House and the Senate in the same form, then being signed by the President, or passed again over a
veto by two-thirds of each chamber (Article I, Section 7). A popular vote isn't one of the steps.
Even the Constitution itself is amended without a national vote: two-thirds of both chambers (or a
convention called by the states) propose an amendment, and three-quarters of the states ratify it (Article V).

**The framers chose representation on purpose.** Writing in 1787 in support of the new Constitution,
James Madison argued that elected representatives would "refine and enlarge the public views"
([The Federalist No. 10](https://constitutioncenter.org/the-constitution/historic-document-library/detail/james-madison-federalist-10-1788)). His view was that a
chosen body could deliberate, weigh competing interests across a large country, and resist the
passions of the moment better than the public voting directly. Not everyone at the time agreed, and
the debate has continued since.

**The volume of decisions is large.** In 2026 alone, through mid-November, the House held more than
320 recorded votes and the Senate more than 260, on this site's records. Many are on amendments,
procedure or nominations, and bills often change through negotiation before a final vote. Members
and their staffs work on this full time.

**Not every federal decision is public law that voters could choose.** The Senate also confirms
judges and officials and approves treaties, which the Constitution assigns to it.

## Where voters do decide laws {#states}

Direct votes on laws do exist in the United States, at the state and local level.

- **26 states** have a statewide process that lets voters propose laws or constitutional amendments
  (an **initiative**), approve or overturn a law the legislature passed (a **referendum**), or both.
  So does Washington, D.C. ([Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/States_with_initiative_or_referendum))
- **South Dakota** was the first state to adopt them, in 1898
  ([Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/History_of_initiative_and_referendum_in_the_U.S.)).
- **Every state but Delaware** requires its voters to approve changes to the state constitution.
- Cities and counties often put questions such as bonds and tax measures to their voters.

This site uses statewide ballot measures as one of its sources: when a state's voters decided a
question that Congress also voted on, the result shows what that state's voters actually chose. See
[Sources](sources.html#state-ballot-measures).

## How voters shape what Congress does {#influence}

- **Elections.** Every House seat is up every two years. Senators serve six-year terms, with about a
  third of the Senate elected every two years. Senators have been elected directly by voters since
  the 17th Amendment (1913); before that, state legislatures chose them.
- **Primaries.** Many members face their strongest challenge from within their own party, so primary
  elections are often where voters have the most say over who serves.
- **Contacting members.** House and Senate offices track the calls, letters and emails they receive,
  by issue. [How to reach yours](#contact).
- **Public opinion.** Members watch polls and surveys of the people they represent, which is what
  this site compares their votes with.

## Contact your members {#contact}

Your two senators and your representative work for everyone where you live, whether or not you voted
for them. Their offices keep count of the calls, emails and letters they get on each issue. If you care
how they vote, tell them: a short message in your
own words, saying where you live and what you'd like them to do, is what offices are set up to hear.

## Should members follow the people they represent? {#follow}

People have disagreed about this for centuries, and this site doesn't take a side. Two classic views
([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/political-representation/)):

- **Delegate:** a representative should vote the way the people they represent want. On this view, a
  vote against a clear majority at home is a failure of representation.
- **Trustee:** a representative should use their own judgment about what's best, even when it differs
  from the majority at home, and answer for it at the next election. The British statesman Edmund
  Burke made this argument in 1774, describing Parliament as "a deliberative assembly of one nation."

Many scholars see most members as somewhere in between: following constituents on issues people
follow closely, and using their own judgment on others. The site shows the comparison; whether a
member should have voted differently is up to you. See also
[Does "voted against the majority" mean a member did something wrong?](faq.html#wrong)

## Could the United States vote on national laws directly? {#national}

Only through a constitutional amendment. Proposals for a national initiative have been introduced in
Congress, in 1907 and again in 1977, and none passed
([Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/National_initiative)).

- **Supporters argue** that it would let voters decide directly when they think Congress isn't
  listening, as voters in states with initiatives already do, and would give voters more reason to
  take part.
- **Opponents argue** that a single yes-or-no vote can't capture complicated legislation, that well-
  funded campaigns can shape the outcome, that a national majority could override smaller states and
  minorities, and that representatives can negotiate and change a bill in ways a ballot can't.

Studies of state initiatives offer evidence on both sides of these arguments, and the debate
continues.
